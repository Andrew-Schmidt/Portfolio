Solar-Powered LoRa Field Node.

Designing a weather-resistant, off-grid mesh node for low-bandwidth communications in remote areas, especially where conventional network coverage is unreliable.

Planned build — not deployedResearch & component selectionPersonal project

Project at a glance

Communication915 MHz LoRa radio using compatible Meshtastic firmware (US region)
PowerSolar charging + rechargeable battery, sized after measurement
InstallationWeather-sealed enclosure, antenna feed-through, elevated mounting

Current status: the concept and component options have been explored. No final bill of materials, measured battery life, field coverage, or completed deployment is claimed.

Why this could be useful

While working in remote broadband service areas, I became interested in low-power, off-grid communication equipment. A network of nodes could be a practical way to experiment with short messages and basic environmental or location telemetry where ordinary connectivity may be limited.

Remote messagingExplore mesh messaging among authorized devices without relying on a cell signal for every hop.
Resilience experimentLearn how node placement, terrain, antenna height, and network density affect coverage.
Technical learningCombine RF propagation, power budgets, solar harvesting, weatherproofing, and Linux/embedded workflows.

Potential field uses are exploratory, not an existing employer-operated network. This is not intended to replace approved dispatch, safety, or emergency communications.

Proposed system architecture

Energy inputSolar panel → compatible charge controller → protected battery
Node electronicsBattery → protected regulator / power rail → LoRa microcontroller + optional sensors
RF link915 MHz transceiver → matched feed-through → external antenna → nearby mesh nodes

Planned build sequence

01
Set requirements. Choose desired message interval, minimum winter autonomy, radio duty cycle, and approved mounting location.
02
Prototype indoors. Confirm the radio board, Meshtastic firmware, antenna, and basic node-to-node messaging before enclosing anything.
03
Measure energy demand. Record sleep, receive, and transmit consumption; calculate average daily watt-hours and battery reserve.
04
Design winter charging. Select the charger, battery chemistry, and below-freezing charge cutoff or appropriately engineered heating arrangement.
05
Test enclosure fit. Mock up battery, controller, glands, antenna connector, ventilation/condensation mitigation, and safe strain relief.
06
Bench and outdoor validation. Test rain resistance, condensation, freeze/thaw exposure, battery voltage, charging behavior, and range from permitted locations.
07
Evaluate a small pilot mesh. Start with a few authorized installations; log connectivity, power reserve, and maintenance needs before expansion.

Questions still to resolve

  • What are the final node board and enclosure dimensions?
  • How much solar generation is achievable during a cloudy Midwest winter?
  • Which safe battery/charger combination remains viable during extreme cold?
  • What range is practical at the selected mounting heights and terrain?
  • What site permissions, RF requirements, and installation rules apply?

A solar-powered Meshtastic node carries low-rate text and telemetry—not broadband internet. Any deployment on utility or employer infrastructure would require explicit authorization. RF settings and antenna choices must comply with applicable rules.
